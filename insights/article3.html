Scotch, Bourbon, and the Rest of the World

Scotch has the law, bourbon has the volume, and Japan has the scarcity. A cask buyer needs all three stories, not one.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 2, 2026 · 6 min read

In a warehouse in Kentucky, there are more barrels of bourbon ageing than there have ever been: 16.1 million of them, counted on 1 January 2025, worth $10 billion on the assessor's books. In Scotland, a single cask of 1975 Ardbeg sold for a reported £16 million. In Japan, a bottle of Yamazaki can command a price that makes both numbers look almost reasonable, because almost none of it exists. Three countries, three completely different scarcities — and a cask buyer who understands only one of them is flying with two instruments covered.

Start with Scotch, because Scotch is the asset class with a rulebook. Under the Scotch Whisky Regulations 2009, Scotch must be produced in Scotland from water and malted barley, distilled below 94.8 percent alcohol by volume, matured in Scotland in oak casks of no more than 700 litres for at least three years, and bottled at no less than 40 percent alcohol. A single malt adds its own discipline: one distillery, pot stills, malted barley only. That legal frame is why a Scotch cask can be described, verified, and trusted across borders — the warehouse, the cask size, and the age statement all mean the same thing in Tokyo as they do in Perth. It is also why Scotch dominates the export trade: £5.4 billion in 2024, the equivalent of 1.4 billion bottles, according to the Scotch Whisky Association, whose chief executive is Mark Kent. The United States, at £971 million, remained the largest market by value; India, at 192 million bottles, the largest by volume. Blended Scotch led at £3.2 billion, or 59.4 percent of exports, while single malt, the cask buyer's usual quarry, fell 17.2 percent to £1.7 billion — a reminder that even inside a growth story, the premium tier can correct.

Then look at bourbon, where the story is volume — and, right now, too much of it. The Kentucky Distillers' Association reported a record 16.1 million bourbon barrels ageing at the start of 2025, plus another million barrels of other spirits, held across a record 127 licensed distilleries run by 103 companies. Ageing-barrel taxes reached $75 million for the year, up 27 percent on 2024 and 163 percent over five years, on an assessed barrel value of $10 billion. The association's president, Eric Gregory, called it a glass-half-full picture: the state also drew a record 2.7 million visitors to the Bourbon Trail in 2024. But the same figures explain why major producers have throttled back — Jim Beam halted production for 2026, and supplier MGP Ingredients saw sales fall 43 percent — and why bulk bourbon prices have softened toward production cost. For a cask buyer, bourbon is the mirror image of Scotch: the law is looser (new charred oak, no minimum age for bourbon itself beyond the "straight" designation's two years), the climate is hotter (evaporation near 6 percent a year against Scotland's 2 percent), and the risk is oversupply rather than scarcity. A Kentucky barrel bought at the top of a glut needs a different thesis from a Speyside hogshead bought into a shortage.

Japan is the third pole, and the strangest. Japanese whisky exports were worth $288 million in 2024 on 12 million litres — down from a $426 million peak in 2022, after a decade in which export value had grown sharply. The average export price was $24 per litre, against an average import price of just $6.60: Japan imports volume and exports prestige. Production is concentrated in a handful of hands — Suntory Holdings and Nikka Whisky, owned by Asahi Breweries, control most capacity and aged stock — and the famous names, Yamazaki, Hakushu, Yoichi, and Miyagikyo, carry age statements that the available old stock cannot support at scale. That concentration is the point for a cask buyer: Japanese casks are rarely offered the way Scotch casks are, the market is thinner, and a single distillery decision can move prices. The trade winds help at the margin — the UK–India agreement cuts Scotch tariffs from 150 percent to 40 percent over ten years, and China halved its Scotch tariff from 10 percent to 5 percent in early 2026 — but those are Scotch tailwinds, not Japanese ones. Japan's scarcity is structural: it comes from stock laid down decades ago, in small quantities, by few makers.

The counterargument deserves a fair hearing, because the tidy three-way split hides real complications. Scotch's legal moat is also a rigidity: the three-year, 700-litre, Scotland-only rules that make a cask verifiable also prevent a distiller from chasing a faster-maturing style or a cheaper warehouse. Bourbon's glut may be exactly what creates its next opportunity, since today's surplus barrels are tomorrow's well-aged stock if demand recovers — and the Bourbon Trail's record visitation suggests the brand itself is not fading. Japan's thin market cuts both ways: scarcity supports prices, but a buyer who cannot resell except through a handful of channels owns a beautiful illiquid. And the export figures warn against reading any single year as a trend: Scotch value fell 3.7 percent in 2024 while volume rose, then fell again in 2025, to £5.36 billion, as a 10 percent US tariff imposed in April 2025 cut American export volume 15 percent between May and December. Tariffs, taxes, and taste move faster than maturation.

Original analysis points to a portfolio conclusion rather than a single-region bet. Scotch casks offer the strongest verification and the deepest resale market, and should anchor a cask holding. Bourbon offers volume and a cyclical entry point — the time to study Kentucky barrels is when barrel taxes are at records and producers are pausing stills, not when everyone is expanding. Japanese whisky offers scarcity value that is genuine but concentrated, best approached through bottles and established names rather than speculative cask claims. A platform that lists all three, with each lot's rules stated in that region's own terms — Scotch age and cask law, bourbon char and fill dates, Japanese distillery and vintage — gives bidders the one thing a single-region dealer cannot: a way to compare unlike things honestly.

The implication for auction design follows. Do not sell "whisky casks" as one product. Sell Scotch casks under Scotch rules, bourbon barrels under bourbon economics, and Japanese stock under its own scarcity logic, each with the figures that govern it: angels' share by climate, duty by jurisdiction, resale depth by region. The buyer who can hold those three stories at once — law, volume, scarcity — is the buyer who will keep bidding when any one story turns. That buyer is the market a cask auction is built for.
