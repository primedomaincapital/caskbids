How Long Before a Cask Pays: The Holding Period Math on Young Whisky

A cask bought for £3,000 costs another £1,800 to £2,400 to keep for twelve years, and the angels take about 2 percent of it every year. The holding period is where cask returns are made — or quietly lost.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

The buyer bought a new-fill cask in 2016 for £3,000. Ten years later he checked the price, and the broker's guide said a twelve-year-old cask from the same distillery was worth £35 to £55 per litre of pure alcohol. His cask held about 100 litres when it was filled. The arithmetic looked generous: a three-thousand-pound cask, ten years on, worth four or five thousand. Then he did the subtraction properly. Storage at £150 a year for ten years. Insurance. A regauge. The angels' share — the evaporation that takes about 2 percent of the spirit every year, so his 200-litre hogshead now held about 163 litres. The gain was real. It was also much smaller than the brochure had implied, and the reason was not the market. It was the calendar. A cask does not appreciate in a vacuum. It appreciates while it costs money every year to keep.

The evaporation is the first cost, and it never stops. The angels' share averages around 2 percent a year in Scotland's cool warehouses, with a range more like 1 to 4 percent year on year, and it can run 5 to 10 percent in the first couple of years of a cask's life. A 200-litre ex-bourbon hogshead filled at new make loses roughly 2 percent of its volume a year — after ten years it holds approximately 163 litres, after twenty years around 134 litres. In Scotland's damp climate the loss tilts toward alcohol rather than water, which means the strength can fall as well as the volume. Kentucky is harsher: 3 to 5 percent a year in the warmer warehouses, which is why a bourbon barrel loses volume faster than a Scotch hogshead. The evaporation is not a defect. It is the mechanism by which the spirit concentrates and matures. But it is also a cost, measured in litres that will never be sold.

The storage bill is the second cost, and it compounds. Warehousing runs £100 to £180 a year at a distillery's own warehouse, £80 to £120 a year at an independent bonded warehouse, and £35 to £200 a year across the market depending on the warehouse and the size of the cask, according to broker Mark Littler. Insurance typically runs 0.5 to 1 percent of declared value, rising as the value appreciates — Littler puts a policy at around £350 for £100,000 of cover. A regauge starts at around £70, a sample the same plus shipping. None of these is large. Added up over a twelve-year hold, the carrying costs routinely reach 60 to 80 percent of the original purchase price. The cask that cost £3,000 to buy costs another £1,800 to £2,400 to keep for twelve years, before insurance, assay testing, or brokerage fees.

The break-even is further away than the brochure says. Work the litres, not the headline. A buyer who purchases 100 litres of pure alcohol at £10 a litre — £1,000 in total — and holds for twelve years at a storage cost of £150 to £200 a cask a year reaches a carrying cost of £1,800 to £2,400 before insurance or brokerage. Accounting for the angels' share reducing the litreage, the break-even price at exit, before any profit, is approximately £28 to £34 per litre of pure alcohol. The market's own figures show the gap: a first-fill ex-bourbon barrel from a sought-after Highland distillery filled in 2024 might trade at £8 to £12 per litre at new-make stage, while the same distillery's twelve-year-old casks command £35 to £55 per litre. The spread exists. The question is whether it is wide enough to cover the twelve years of bills in between — and for many casks, the answer is narrower than the sales pitch allows.

The market has already cooled. CaskID, a cask registration and valuation service, estimated in October 2026 that comparable Scotch cask prices in 2024 to 2026 ran about 28 percent below their 2020 to 2023 level, based on 767 transactions. The Rare Whisky 101 Apex 1000 index has shown annualised returns of approximately 12 percent over the past decade, and realistic annualised returns for well-sourced casks from reputable distilleries have historically ranged between 8 and 15 percent a year — but those figures require significant qualification, because they describe the best-sourced casks, not the average. The Scotch Whisky Association is blunt about the structure: there is no regulated market, no official price list, and no established way to sell. A cask is worth what the next buyer will pay for it, and the next buyer is not obliged to appear on schedule.

Time is the filter, not the guarantee. Spirit must mature at least three years to be sold as Scotch, and Which? reports that prices tend to rise significantly only once a cask has rested 12 to 18 years. Mark Littler's guidance is plainer still: if you have had the cask less than three years and it is under twelve years old, it is likely worth around what you paid for it, barring huge market shifts. If you are 15 to 20 years in, the market changes over that time mean you should be up — but the change in value between 3 and 15 to 20 years is not linear, so there are no uniform year-on-year increases. New-make spirit usually ranges from £2,000 to £5,000 a cask; young casks of five to ten years often cost £4,000 to £6,000. The first meaningful result is a long way off, and the realistic floor is losing most of what was paid — which is what happened to many buyers from the firms that collapsed.

The counterargument. The case for the long hold is not fantasy. Some casks really have risen sharply, and the mechanism is real: a closed distillery cannot make more spirit, a sought-after distillery's new-make price rises every year, and a twelve-year-old cask from a name the market wants is a different asset from a three-year-old cask from a name it does not. The Knight Frank Luxury Investment Index has consistently ranked rare whisky among the top-performing collectible categories. Glenfarclas, Springbank, and a selection of closed distilleries saw per-litre prices escalate far beyond what the maturation mathematics alone would predict, driven by demand from East Asia and the United States outpacing supply. The buyers who made money did not buy time. They bought scarcity, and time delivered it.

That defence has a limit, and the limit is the one the brochures skip. The returns came from distillery reputation inflation — the revaluation of certain producers — not from the maturation premium itself, and reputation is the part of the thesis that cannot be bought at any price. A new-make cask from an unfashionable distillery, held for twelve years and stored correctly, is still a cask from an unfashionable distillery. The holding period that pays is the one where the distillery's name did the work while the warehouse did the waiting. Buy the name first, count the storage second, and treat any return before year twelve as a rumour rather than a plan.

A cask pays when the distillery's reputation, the litreage left after evaporation, and the storage bill all point the same way — and that takes longer than the sales pitch admits.
