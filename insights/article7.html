The Buyer's Premium, Priced Out: What a Winning Bid Really Costs

The hammer fell at £20,000. The invoice was £23,600. The buyer's premium — 15 percent plus VAT at the specialist houses, 24 percent at the big rooms — is the part of the price most bidders never count until it arrives.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

The hammer fell at £20,000. The room — a browser tab, in this case, the auction long since moved online — showed the winning bid in green, and for about ten seconds the buyer felt like the owner of a twelve-year-old Speyside hogshead. Then the invoice arrived. Hammer price £20,000. Buyer commission £3,000. VAT on the commission £600. Delivery, insurance while the cask moved warehouses, and a regauge to confirm what was actually inside. The cask the buyer thought he had bought for twenty thousand pounds had cost him close to twenty-four thousand before it had moved an inch. Nothing in the invoice was hidden. Every line had been published on the auction house's fee page. He simply had not read it as a price list. He had read the hammer as the price.

The premium, house by house. The buyer's premium is the auction house's cut from the buyer's side, and it varies more than most bidders expect. At Whisky Auctioneer, the largest online whisky auction house, the published buyer commission is 15 percent of the hammer price, with 20 percent VAT charged on the commission itself — which means the commission and its VAT together add 18 percent to the hammer price. There is no VAT on the hammer price itself. Scotch Whisky Auctions charges the same way: 15 percent commission, VAT on top. Whisky Hammer charges buyers 15 percent as well, and charges sellers nothing — no seller commission at all, only a £7 bottle registration fee plus VAT. The pattern is consistent across the specialist houses: the buyer pays roughly a sixth on top of the hammer, and the seller's side is where the houses compete.

The number moved, and the old guides did not. Older guides still quote Whisky Auctioneer's buyer premium at 12.5 percent, and that figure was accurate when those guides were written. The house's own published fee page, checked on November 1, 2026, lists 15 percent. A bidder working from a 12.5 percent figure underprices the bill by two and a half points on every lot — £500 on a £20,000 hammer. The lesson is not that any house is hiding its fees. It is that auction fees move, and the only fee schedule that matters is the one published the week you bid. Check it before every auction, not once a year.

Outside the specialists, the premium jumps. The big international houses charge more. Sotheby's lists a 24 percent buyer's premium for its Global Wines & Spirits sales, plus an overhead premium on top. Bonhams in the UK lists 24 percent for wine and whisky. The smaller online platforms charge less: WhiskyAuction.com, running since 1997, charges buyers 10 percent and sellers 10 percent. Whiskey Bidders, the Irish platform, charges a 10 percent premium plus VAT at the Irish rate — on a €1,000 hammer, the premium is €100 and the VAT on it €23, for a total of €1,123 before delivery. The spread between the cheapest and the most expensive room is fourteen points. On a £50,000 cask, that is £7,000 of difference before a single other cost is counted.

VAT is the part most buyers get wrong. There are two different VAT treatments in a whisky auction, and confusing them is expensive. The hammer price itself carries no VAT — it is the price agreed between buyer and seller. The commission does carry VAT, at 20 percent in the UK. So the arithmetic on a £20,000 hammer at a 15 percent house is: £3,000 commission, £600 VAT on that commission, £23,600 before anything else. A buyer who assumes VAT applies to the whole invoice overstates the bill by £4,000. A buyer who assumes there is no VAT at all understates it by £600. Both mistakes are common, and both are avoidable by reading one line of the fee page.

Then the rest of the bill arrives. Delivery is the next line, and it is priced per bottle rather than per cask in most fee schedules — £13.20 for one bottle within the UK at both Whisky Auctioneer and Scotch Whisky Auctions, £25.20 for six bottles together at Scotch Whisky Auctions. A whole cask does not ship like six bottles, and the delivery and transfer of a cask is arranged separately, with the warehouse — which is why cask buyers should confirm the transfer cost with the warehousekeeper before bidding, not after. Whisky Auctioneer also offers optional loss and breakage cover at 4 percent of the hammer price, and charges a one-off £5 lifetime registration fee. None of these is large on its own. Together they are the difference between the hammer and the money that actually leaves the account.

The worked example. Take a £20,000 hammer on a twelve-year-old Speyside hogshead at Whisky Auctioneer. Commission at 15 percent: £3,000. VAT on the commission at 20 percent: £600. Registration: £5. Loss and breakage cover, if taken, at 4 percent: £800. Delivery and cask transfer: arranged with the warehouse, typically a few hundred pounds. The all-in cost lands between £23,600 and £24,700, depending on cover and transfer. The same hammer at Whisky Hammer costs £23,000 plus delivery and the £7 registration, because the seller pays the commission there and the buyer pays 15 percent — the same buyer rate, a different split. The same hammer at Sotheby's costs £24,800 before the overhead premium. The hammer is the same number in all three rooms. The bill is not. The delivery charge is the one line the houses leave to the warehouse, and it is worth confirming before the bid rather than after the invoice arrives.

The counterargument. The premium is not a tax on enthusiasm; it is what pays for the room. The houses that charge it provide the authentication, the escrow, the global bidder pool, and the dispute process that make a remote purchase possible at all. Whisky Auctioneer, founded in Perth in 2013 by Iain McClune, has sold more than 750,000 lots to 70,000 clients in more than 80 countries, taking 150,000 bids a month. That reach is the product, and the premium is its price. A private sale has no premium — and no escrow, no authentication, and no recourse when the cask is not what the seller said it was. The buyers who complain loudest about the premium are often the ones who have never tried to sell a cask without a room behind them.

That defence has a limit, and it is the limit that matters. A premium is fair when it is published, stable, and the same for everyone in the room. It stops being fair when it moves without notice, when the VAT treatment is buried, or when the buyer discovers it on the invoice rather than before the bid. The houses publish their fees. The buyer's job is to read them as a price list, not as fine print — and to bid the all-in number. The arithmetic is simple: divide your budget by 1.18 before you bid at a 15 percent house, and the hammer you can afford is the number that appears. Bid the hammer, and the premium bids you.
