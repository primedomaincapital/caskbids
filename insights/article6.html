How to Sell a Whisky Cask: Auctions, Brokers, and What the Hammer Takes

The hammer price is not your money. Seller commissions run from zero to more than 20 percent, the buyer pays a premium on top, and the warehouse paperwork decides whether the sale happens at all.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 15, 2026 · 7 min read

The seller had owned the cask for eleven years. It sat in a bonded warehouse in Speyside, gaining a year of age with every January, losing about 2 percent a year to the angels’ share. When the hammer fell, the price looked like a triumph — until the statement arrived. The commission, the VAT on the commission, and three weeks of waiting for the money turned a headline number into a smaller one. Selling a cask is not the mirror image of buying one. The buyer worries about provenance. The seller worries about the net.

The three routes. Auctions are the most visible route, and the largest room is online. Whisky Auctioneer, founded in Perth in 2013 by Iain McClune, has sold more than 750,000 lots, takes more than 150,000 bids a month, and serves buyers in more than eighty countries. Just Whisky and Prestige Whisky Auction run the same online model with different fee terms, and the traditional houses — Sotheby’s and Christie’s — still set the records, including two Karuizawa casks at £2,125,000 each in March 2026. Brokers are the second route: Cask Trade, Mark Littler, and the 1901 Group all hold stock and publish guide ranges, and a broker’s job on the sell side is private introductions, managed negotiation, and paperwork handled for a combined fee. The third route is direct — selling to an independent bottler, which avoids third-party auction and broker commissions and can produce a faster outcome, or back to a distillery. About 22 million casks are maturing in Scotland at any given time, according to the Scotch Whisky Association. The buyers are there. The question is which door you walk through, and what it charges.

What the hammer takes. This is the part sellers most often skip, and it is the whole article. Whisky Auctioneer charges sellers 5% of the hammer price, with buyers paying a 12.5% premium on top, and payment by bank transfer within 21 working days — those are the platform’s published terms as reported in a 2026 review, and cask-specific terms should be confirmed in writing before consigning, because cask lots are not bottle lots. Just Whisky charges 0% seller’s commission on casks — the full hammer price is the seller’s, the buyers pay the premium, and payment typically lands within 21 working days. Prestige Whisky Auction also charges 0% seller fees on casks, and aims to pay within 24 working hours. Mark Littler’s sell-side guide puts the honest range on the record: seller commissions on cask auction platforms vary from 0 to over 20% plus VAT of hammer price, with buyer premiums of 10 to 25% plus VAT on top, plus listing, reserve, and registration fees at some houses. Traditional auction houses typically charge the seller 10 to 15%. The arithmetic on a £100,000 hammer price: at 0% the seller keeps £100,000; at 5% it is £95,000; at 20% it is £80,000 — before VAT on the fees and any listing or reserve charges. A higher headline through a high-commission route can net less than a lower headline through a free one.

The buyer’s premium is your problem too. A premium the buyer pays is money the buyer cannot bid. The 1988 refill butt that sold at Whisky Hammer in 2022 went for £915,500 on the hammer and £1,007,050 once the buyer’s premium was added — 10% the buyer paid on top, which is also 10% the buyer could not put into the bid. High buyer premiums suppress hammer prices, which is why the seller’s net depends on both sides of the fee schedule, not just the commission line. And the fees do not all vanish when a lot fails to sell: if a bottle or cask carries a reserve and does not meet it, listing and reserve fees still apply, so a reserve set on hope rather than evidence costs money even when nothing changes hands.

Getting paid. The second number that matters, after the net, is the wait. Prestige Whisky Auction aims to pay within 24 working hours of the sale. Whisky Auctioneer and Just Whisky pay within 21 working days. Traditional auction houses typically pay four to six weeks after the sale closes. Three weeks against six is not a detail when the money is five or six figures and the warehouse rent keeps running. Ask for the payout window in writing before the lot goes in, and treat a vague answer as information.

The paperwork that closes the sale. A cask sale closes on documents, not handshakes. The Delivery Order is the primary transfer document for any Scotch whisky cask sale — it is what moves ownership in the warehouse ledger. A fresh re-gauge matters as much on the way out as on the way in: Prestige requires the re-gauge within six months of the planned auction date, because the volume on the certificate is from the last gauge and the angels’ share takes roughly 2 percent a year. Not every warehouse will cooperate: a cask auction only works if the warehouse will open new accounts for private owners and allows cask sales via auction, so confirm that before choosing the route. On tax, the position is kinder than most sellers expect: a cask sold whole in bond carries no excise duty or VAT at the point of sale, and HMRC treats whisky casks as wasting assets, which means personal cask sales are typically exempt from capital gains tax. Confirm both points with your own adviser — “typically” is doing work in that sentence — but the structure of the sale is the seller’s friend, provided the sale stays in bond.

Pricing it. A seller who prices from the record results will wait a long time. The honest anchors are the broker guide ranges: Cask Trade lists whole-cask entry prices from £1,500; the 1901 Group ranges newly filled casks at £3,000 to £8,000, three to five years at £5,000 to £15,000, eight to twelve years at £15,000 to £40,000, mature premium at £40,000 to £100,000 and above, and rare or collectible casks at £100,000 and beyond; Mark Littler puts new-make spirit from about £2,000 for a barrel, £2,500 for a hogshead, and £4,500 for a butt, with young casks of four to twelve years at £4,000 to £8,000. There is no verified, public, current whole-cask price index — only auction results at the top, guide ranges in the middle, and bottled indices that track a different asset. Knight Frank’s rare-whisky index fell 9 percent in 2024 and sat 19.3 percent below its summer 2022 peak, with bottled prices down a further 10.9 percent in the final quarter of 2025. Those are bottle prices. Reading them as cask prices is the most expensive mistake in this market, and it is made most often by sellers.

The counterargument. Auctions are not always the best route, and the trade says so plainly. Mark Littler’s sell-side guide warns that cask auctions remain niche because of the complications of transferring ownership at the warehouse level, and that buyer and seller commissions mean selling a cask at auction can often underperform — in 2026, the guide says, an auction would not be the suggested route for a sale without proper consultation. A broker introduction or a direct sale to a bottler can net more on a lower headline, with less waiting and no reserve fee if the deal fails. There is a second reason to prepare properly: reports of alcohol investment fraud in Britain have more than tripled in four years, and buyers now demand the same verification on the way out that sellers demanded on the way in. A seller who cannot produce warehouse records, a fresh re-gauge, and a clean delivery order will not get a bid at any price. In this market, the paperwork is the product.

Price the net, not the hammer. Get the full fee schedule — commission, buyer premium, listing, reserve, VAT — in writing before the cask is consigned. Compare the net across auction, broker, and direct routes on the same hammer price, not the headline each route promises. Set the reserve from the guide ranges, not the record results. And have the delivery order and a fresh re-gauge ready before the listing goes live, because the buyer who has to wait for proof is the buyer who bids somewhere else. The cask spent years gaining value in the dark. The sale should not give it away in the light.
